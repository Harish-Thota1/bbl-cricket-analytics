## The problem

Before a T20 match a coach decides which bowlers to use against the opposition's best
batters. That decision is usually made from memory. This project answers it from data:
pick a batter, see which bowlers hold him back and which he scores freely against.

## The metric everything rests on

A batter scoring 104 runs per 100 balls against a bowler tells you nothing on its own. Is
that good bowling, or a slow batter?

Aaron Finch's career rate is 126. So 104 against one bowler means that bowler slows him by
about a fifth. A batter whose normal rate *is* 104 shows no effect at all.

Every figure is therefore a **ratio**: his rate against one bowler, divided by his own
career rate. Finch against a bowler who holds him to 104 gives 104 ÷ 126 = 0.82.

| Ratio | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0.73 | 27% slower than his normal — the bowler has the edge |
| 1.00 | no effect |
| 1.15 | 15% faster than his normal — the batter has the edge |

This separates the bowler's effect from the batter's style, and is the single idea that
distinguishes this from a statistics table.

## Validation approach

Two kinds of check, both required.

**Relative checks** compare one stage of the pipeline to the stage before it. Does the
fact table have the same number of rows as the raw table? Does every player ID in the fact
table exist in the player table? These catch anything lost or broken in transit.

**Absolute checks** compare the data to the outside world. A T20 match has about 240
deliveries. Eight teams play in the BBL. A batter cannot score more than six off one ball.
These catch errors that are present at every stage and would therefore satisfy any relative
check.

An early version of this pipeline loaded every ball twice. The relative check passed
happily: both stages agreed at 306,500 rows, because both were doubled. What caught it was
cricket knowledge. Every stage now carries at least one absolute check; the strongest is
§5b, which compares a computed figure to a published statistic.

## §1 · Loading the raw data

### The duplication bug

Cricsheet ships `all_matches.csv` alongside the 662 individual match files. It contains
every delivery already present in those files. A naive folder read loads both.

**Nothing errors, and most numbers still look right.** Strike rate is runs divided by
balls; if both double, the division cancels and the answer is unchanged. Averages, economy
rates and percentages all behave the same way.

What breaks is the **counts**. Every matchup appears to rest on twice the evidence it
really has — a pairing showing 60 balls actually has 30, and a dashboard promising "60+
ball matchups" is quietly making a claim the data does not support.

**Fix:** keep only numerically-named files (`1023581.csv`). `files` is defined once, in one
cell. Redefining it later with an unfiltered glob is how the duplication returned after the
first fix.

**Detection:** balls per match. 231 is a T20; 463 is not.

### Verify at the destination, not the source

During development, `len(raw)` in Python printed 153,250 while the database table still
held 306,500 rows. The `to_sql` call had not taken effect, and nothing said so.

The lesson generalises: **checking the object you sent proves nothing about the table you
sent it to.** Every count check in this pipeline queries the database, not the dataframe
that was written to it.

### Why the duplicate tolerance is 2, not 0

A first uniqueness check used match, innings, ball, batter, bowler and demanded zero
duplicates. It flagged 5 rows. Four were legitimate:

| Match | Ball | The two rows | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 654033 | 10.1 | a wide, then 2 runs | legitimate |
| 756749 | 0.1 | a wide, then a dot | legitimate |
| 1443088 | 1.1 | a dot, then a wide | legitimate |
| 1269692 | 11.1 | a four and a six, no extra flagged | odd but real |
| **571256** | **4.1** | **identical wide recorded twice** | **genuine duplicate** |

A wide or no-ball is **re-bowled**, so the illegal delivery and its replacement share a
ball number, batter and bowler. Adding runs and extras to the key separates them.

The fifth is real — one row in 153,250 (0.0007%). **Left in place and documented rather
than deleted.** Removing source rows to make a test pass hides a problem instead of
recording it.

Both failure directions matter. The earlier check was too loose and missed a genuine
doubling; this one was too tight and flagged normal cricket. Checks have to be tuned
against the sport, and the tolerance of 2 is a stated decision, not a number chosen to make
a test go green.

## §2 · Venues

32 names in the raw data, **21 actual grounds**. Stadiums get renamed when sponsors change,
and the data records whatever the ground was called that day. Left alone, "how do teams
score at the Gabba" splits across three buckets.

Most merges are obvious — "Bellerive Oval" and "Bellerive Oval, Hobart". Two share no
common text and were verified against published records:

- **Aurora Stadium = University of Tasmania Stadium.** Both York Park, Launceston. Aurora
  Energy held naming rights 2004–2016; the University took over January 2017.
  *(Wikipedia, York Park)*
- **Simonds Stadium = GMHBA Stadium.** Both Kardinia Park, South Geelong. Full history:
  Shell 1999–2001, Baytec 2001, Skilled 2001–2011, Simonds 2012–2017, GMHBA 2017–.
  *(Kardinia Park Stadium Trust)*

**Deliberately not merged:** Perth Stadium and Stadium Australia. Similar wording,
different cities.

No text matching would have found the sponsor renames. That needed an external source.

## §3 · Why the match columns are not "home" and "away"

An earlier version labelled the two teams `team_home` and `team_away`. That was an
assumption, and it was wrong about half the time.

Measured across all 662 matches:

| Venue | Home side batted first |
|---|---|
| Adelaide Oval | 51% |
| MCG | 48% |
| WACA | 57% |

Before every match there is a **coin toss**, and the winning captain chooses to bat or
bowl. That choice is tactical and essentially independent of who is at home.

The raw data contains no home/away information. The columns are named for what it provably
holds: who batted first, who bowled first. A label that is wrong half the time is worse
than no label, because a reader would trust it.

## §4 · Player identity

The same player can be written different ways; different players can be written the same
way. Getting it wrong silently merges two careers or splits one.

Cricsheet's match files carry a **registry** giving every player a permanent ID (linked to
ESPNCricinfo). Identity comes from that ID, never from the name.

### Two cases that prove it

| Names | Registry | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| C Green / CJ Green | **different IDs** | two players, kept apart |
| J Brown / Josh Brown | **same ID** | one player, merged |

Both pairs looked identical by commonsense tests — similar names, never in the same match,
careers in different periods. **The registry resolved them in opposite directions.**

### Limit of the same-match test

Two names never appearing in one match can *disprove* a merge — one person cannot bat under
two names in a game. It cannot *prove* one: two different players may simply never have
coexisted. Used only as a consistency check on the registry, never as evidence of identity.

### The join-safety check

`fact_delivery` looks up each ball's batter and bowler **by name**. If the name "M Smith"
appeared in the registry twice, attached to two different IDs, that lookup would match both
rows — and every ball M Smith ever faced would land in the fact table twice. No error, no
warning, just a player whose record is silently doubled.

An earlier version of this pipeline checked the reverse: **one ID holding two names**,
which is how the J Brown / Josh Brown merge was found. That is a useful check, but it finds
a different problem. Two names under one ID means a player was recorded inconsistently.
**One name under two IDs is what duplicates rows** — and nothing was looking for it.

The check runs before the fact table is built and must return zero. If it ever returns a
name, that name needs resolving by hand before the pipeline continues.

## §5 · The fact table

One row per delivery. Every column is either a **link** to a dimension or a **fact** about
that ball.

### `bowler_wicket`

True only when the bowler earned the dismissal. Run-outs, retirements and obstruction are
excluded — a run-out is a fielding mix-up, not something the bowler did. Counting them
would overstate every bowler's record, and a cricket person would spot it immediately.

### `phase`

| Phase | Overs | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Powerplay | 0–5 | fielding restrictions, batters attack |
| Middle | 6–14 | fields spread, scoring slows |
| Death | 15–19 | all-out hitting |

Computed once rather than recalculated in every query.

### §5b · The external check

Chris Lynn's BBL strike rate is publicly ~145. If the pipeline computes 145, the whole
chain works.

**The ball count is checked too, and that is the point.** A doubled dataset would still
show 145 — runs and balls both double, the ratio cancels — but the career ball count would
be impossible. Checking both together catches what neither catches alone, and that is
exactly the error that got through the first time.

## §6 · Bowling styles

### Why it matters

Knowing a batter struggles against one bowler is only useful if you have that bowler.
Knowing he struggles against **leg spin** works against any opponent who has one.

It also fixes the biggest weakness in the data. One batter against one bowler is 30–90
balls. Group all leg-spinners and the same batter has faced 400+. The numbers stop being
indicative and start being solid.

### The problem

**Cricsheet carries no player attributes at all.** Verified by reading a complete match
info file: teams, venue, toss, squads, player registry. Nothing else.

### Routes considered

| Route | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Scraping ESPNCricinfo | **Rejected.** Returns 403 to scripted requests — it does not permit automated access. Getting past that would mean impersonating a browser at the TLS level, which is not appropriate for a published project. |
| Wikipedia public API | Permitted and works, but requires each player's full name — meaning a manual lookup per player anyway, so no time saved. |
| **`cricketdata` R package** | **Used.** Maintained CRAN package with `fetch_player_meta()`, built for this purpose. |

The R route connects cleanly to data already present: Cricsheet publishes a `people.csv`
register mapping its player IDs to ESPNCricinfo IDs, which is what `fetch_player_meta()`
takes.

**Result: all 372 bowlers classified. No manual lookups, no scraping.**

### Why the pipeline is two commands, not one

Section 6 requires a separate R step. That is a documented constraint of the data
landscape, not an unfinished implementation — bowling style exists in no source the
pipeline can reach automatically, as the table above shows.

The notebook writes `fetch_styles.R` so the step runs as `Rscript fetch_styles.R` rather
than an interactive session: reproducible, one command, no copy-paste.

Calling R from Python with `subprocess` would make it *appear* to be one step, but adds a
hidden dependency on R being installed and on PATH, and fails obscurely when it is not. The
explicit two-command sequence is more honest about what the pipeline needs.

### Grouping decisions

**"Legbreak" and "Legbreak googly" both become Leg spin.** A googly is a variation within
leg spin — the same bowler bowls both.

**"fast-medium" and "medium-fast" are equivalent.** The distinction is marginal and applied
inconsistently by whoever writes the profiles.

An `assert` halts the notebook if an unmapped style appears, so a new value cannot slip
through unclassified.

### Reality check on the result

Expected shape: pace-dominated, spin a minority, left-arm wrist spin rare. More
leg-spinners than pace bowlers would mean the mapping is wrong regardless of what any
technical check said.

## §7 · The serving views

All aggregation lives in SQL. Power BI imports and displays. The logic stays readable,
testable and version-controlled outside a `.pbix` file, and Power BI receives a few hundred
summarised rows instead of 153,250 raw ones.

### §7a · `v_scouting` — page 1

Built in three stages, shown separately in the notebook so the logic is visible:

1. **baseline** — one row per batter, his career norms
2. **matchup** — one row per pairing, the same measures against one bowler
3. **join and divide** — the two side by side, producing the ratios

Stage 2's numbers mean nothing alone: a strike rate of 106 could be containment or could be
that batter's ordinary rate. Stage 3 is where a number becomes a finding.

### Why 30 balls — measured, not assumed

| Threshold | Pairings | Batters |
|---|---|---|
| 60 balls | 37 | 16 — too few to build anything |
| **30 balls** | **620** | **73** |

Thirty balls is about five overs of head-to-head. A stated trade-off: enough for a pattern
to be worth noticing, not enough for certainty. Over 10 balls a batter might score 4 runs
or 20 purely by chance, and either would look like a pattern.

**Whatever `MATCHUP_MIN_BALLS` is set to must be the number printed on the dashboard.** A
check enforces it, so the caption cannot drift out of step with the data — the kind of
error a domain expert spots in ten seconds.

### Three ratios, not one

`sr_ratio` says a batter is being slowed. It does not say how.

**`dot_ratio`** — his share of scoreless balls against this bowler ÷ his usual share. Above
1.00 means tied down more than normal. Dots matter because a batter who cannot score for
three balls tends to take a risk on the fourth.

**`boundary_ratio`** — his share of fours and sixes ÷ his usual share. Lynn hits a boundary
off roughly 22% of balls faced; if that drops to 15% against one bowler, the ratio is 0.68
— that bowler is denying him the big shots even while he still finds singles.

Containment happens two ways. One bowler starves a batter of singles; another lets him
rotate but denies boundaries. Different field settings, and `sr_ratio` alone cannot tell
them apart.

### Coverage

Only batters with 500+ career balls get a baseline. A career rate from a handful of innings
is unreliable, and since every ratio is divided by it, an unreliable baseline makes every
ratio unreliable.

**Two numbers describe the coverage, and they say opposite things:** the threshold excludes
most individual players, but keeps the players who face most of the deliveries. The second
is the meaningful one — a tail-ender who faced forty balls does not need a scouting report.

**Limitation:** this describes established batters only. It says nothing about emerging or
lower-order players, a genuine gap in a talent-pathway context.

### §7c · `v_bowler_type` — page 2

Page 1 tells a coach to bowl one particular bowler at one particular batter. That only
helps if you have that bowler. This view groups bowlers by type, so the answer works
against any opponent — and multiplies the evidence from 30–90 balls to 400+.

`sample_confidence` marks each row *solid* (200+ balls), *moderate* (100–199) or *thin*
(under 100). The dashboard fades the thin ones, because on screen a row built on 80 balls
looks just as trustworthy as one built on 400.

**The Lynn result is worth reading carefully.** The obvious conclusion is "he struggles
against spin" — but off spin is his best type while leg spin is his worst, and both are
spin. The pattern is **which way the ball turns**: leg spin and left-arm orthodox both turn
away from a right-hander; off spin turns into him.

That is a cricket insight, not a statistical one. No model would have labelled it, because
it needs someone to notice that two of six categories share a physical property.

### §7d · `v_death_bowling` — page 3

A different question: not "who do we bowl at this batter" but "who bowls the 19th over".
The last five overs decide T20 matches, and the call is made in seconds.

**Economy** is runs per over, extras included — a wide costs the bowling side a run
regardless of who bowled it.

**Dot ball percentage** is the share of deliveries conceding nothing. Dots create the
wicket even when the dot-baller is not the one who takes it.

⚠️ **`team_ref` is a rough guess at each bowler's team.** The data never says which club a
player belongs to, so the team is taken from whichever side bowled first in his matches.
That works most of the time, but a player who moved clubs will appear twice, once under
each. Use it to narrow a long list down to roughly one squad — not to answer "who plays for
the Strikers".

## §8 · Power BI

**Connect:** Get Data → PostgreSQL database → `<host>:5432`, database `bbl`, **Import**.
Import **only the four views** — not `fact_delivery`, which is 153,000 rows already
summarised.

| View | Page | Drives |
|---|---|---|
| `v_scouting` | 1 | batter slicer, bowl / avoid tables |
| `v_dismissals` | 1 | how he gets out |
| `v_bowler_type` | 2 | performance by bowling type |
| `v_death_bowling` | 3 | death-over economy |

**Relationships.** Link `v_dismissals[batter]` and `v_bowler_type[batter]` to
`v_scouting[batter]` so one batter selection drives pages 1 and 2. Page 3 is bowler-level
and stays independent.

**Sync slicers** across pages 1 and 2 only. Page 3 uses a team slicer.

**The relationship trap.** Power BI allows only one *active* relationship between two
tables. Import `dim_player` and link it to both `batter_id` and `bowler_id`, and one
becomes inactive — that filter then silently stops working, with no warning. The four views
avoid this by carrying player names directly.

**Set every numeric field to "Don't summarize".** Power BI defaults to Sum, which is
meaningless for a ratio and produces a nonsense total row.

**Data bar limitation.** Power BI allows one bar colour per column, so bars cannot be green
below 1.00 and red above. Use a neutral bar and let conditionally-coloured text carry the
direction.

**Footer on every page** — the wording matters as much as the numbers:

> Ratio = strike rate against this bowler ÷ the batter's career strike rate. Under 1.00
> means he scores slower than his own normal. 30+ ball matchups only; dimmed rows are thin
> samples. Not controlled for phase or venue.

## §9 · Limitations

A finding without its limits is not a finding.

### Phase — tested, not assumed

A bowler used mainly in the powerplay looks more suppressive regardless of skill, because
scoring is naturally slower then. The obvious correction is to compare each matchup against
the batter's baseline *for that phase*.

That was measured. At the 30-ball threshold it leaves:

| Phase | Matchups | Batters |
|---|---|---|
| Middle | 109 | 40 |
| Powerplay | 73 | 29 |
| **Death** | **6** | **5** |

**188 matchups instead of 620** — and effectively nothing in the death overs, the phase
that decides T20 matches. T20 head-to-head samples cannot support the split.

The confound is therefore **acknowledged rather than corrected**, which is the honest
position: the correction exists, it was tried, and the data does not have the depth for it.
**These are associations, not causes.**

### Other limits

**Not controlled for venue, match situation or era.** Fourteen seasons of a changing
format.

**Bowling style is one label per bowler.** A bowler who varies pace, bowls cutters, or
changed style mid-career is reduced to a single category.

**Samples are small by nature.** T20 head-to-head records rarely exceed sixty balls. At
thirty an individual pairing is indicative, not conclusive. No significance testing has
been applied; a proportion test is the obvious next step.

**"Toughest" and "easiest" are extremes by construction.** For each batter these are the
most extreme of roughly twenty candidates, so some will be ordinary variation.

**Player identity depends on the Cricsheet registry.** Treated as authoritative — it links
to ESPNCricinfo — but not independently audited across all players.

**One known artefact remains.** Match 571256, ball 4.1: an identical wide recorded twice.
One row in 153,250, documented rather than deleted.

**This is men's BBL.** The pipeline is competition-agnostic — the Women's Big Bash uses the
identical Cricsheet format, so pointing the loader at it is a data swap rather than a
rebuild. That reusability is the design goal.

## Tried and rejected

Four approaches assessed and set aside, with reasons:

| Approach | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Scraping ESPNCricinfo for bowling styles** | Returns 403 to scripted requests. Getting past it means impersonating a browser at the TLS level — circumventing a control the site put there deliberately. |
| **Wikipedia API for bowling styles** | Permitted and functional, but requires each player's full name. Finding the full name means opening the profile, at which point the style is already visible. No time saved. |
| **Phase-adjusted baselines** | Removes a real confound, but drops 620 matchups to 188 and leaves 6 in the death overs. The correction costs more than it buys. |
| **Manual style mapping for the top 20 bowlers** | Would have covered ~28% of deliveries. Superseded by the R package, which covered all 372 bowlers with no manual work. |

Each was tested rather than dismissed. The phase result in particular is reported in §9
with its numbers, because a measured rejection is stronger evidence of judgement than an
unexamined limitation.

## What this cannot replicate

Stated plainly, because overclaiming is the fastest way to lose credibility with someone
who does the job:

- **Match-day live coding** — tagging every ball as it happens, at the ground, with video
  software. A real-time skill, not a desk one.
- **Video analysis** — this data shows *that* a batter struggles against a bowling type. It
  cannot show *how*, and a coach fixes technique they can see.
- **Fielding and workload data** — not present in ball-by-ball records.
- **Multiple disagreeing sources** — one clean feed here; a working environment means
  several systems that do not share player IDs.

The identity work in §4 is the closest analogue to that last problem, and the part most
likely to transfer.
